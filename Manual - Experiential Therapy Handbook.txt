Welcome!
In this manual, I will take you through the main stages of experiential work and
introduce you to the types of question you might ask at each step. Please use
this as a reference guide that orients you to all the pieces of clinical work worth
learning about and as a jumping board for further research / training. This
manual is not meant as a full (or vetted) training material related to the three
therapies it draws from. But I hope it is helpful to those of us working to
integrate between them.
I will be connecting the question not only to ‘steps of the work,’ but also to the
four main pieces/types of work, as outlined in a diagram below that I crafted for
my students.
The bottom 2 quadrants are pieces of clinical work that set the stage for the
deeper healing work. And the top 2 quadrants are the two paths of deeper
work itself. It is these two deeper pieces of work that often lead to
juxtapositions, especially if they are paired (more on next page).

The 4 C’s of Experiential Work
CONTACT is all about finding the right observing distance (ie a mindful,
regulated, resourced, curious stance). We don’t need perfect “Self energy” (an
IFS term), but we do need enough.
CLARITY is all about keeping things organized in terms of the various
elements that emerge (being able to make discernments between different
parts or issues or feelings, so that one piece of work can be focused on with
clarity and precision). This is related to concept of ‘Therapeutic Focus.’
Once the stage is set by the foundation of both CONTACT and CLARITY (ie
you’ve helped the client enter a mindful, compassionate witnessing of a very
specific part of their experience), then the top 2 C’s become possible. Those
are:
COHERENCE is all about understanding the coherence of the feeling /
impulse / pattern. In other words why it makes sense based on one’s
unconscious world of meanings (ie schemas).
COMPLETION is about completing unfinished emotional ‘work’ that got
blocked. This could be working through unprocessed grief, or anger, or selfprotection. Completion work is not always necessary, but is a very common
and powerful way of creating disconfirmations, since so many of our schemas
are around not being able to complete a healthy response. Often our old
wounds are around blocked healthy emotional responses (like crying, using
anger to set boundaries, etc.), and the subsequent schemas we pick up that
our healthy responses are dangerous, or at least pointless. And one way to
disconfirm this is through a new experience of completing, rather than
defending against, them (our emotions, impulses, activations).

OTHER TERMS USED IN MANUAL
COHERENCE THERAPY TERMS:
SCHEMA (also called ‘emotional learning’) is a prediction held in implicit
memory (subcortically) around what things mean for our safety or wellbeing, as
well as how we should respond to protect ourselves.
JUXTAPOSITIONS occur when our brain simultaneously witnesses two
predictions / knowings / ‘truths’ that can’t BOTH be true (one ‘disconfirms’ the
other).
MEMORY RECONSOLIDATION is the brain’s innate mechanism for erasing
or updating old schemas / emotional learnings. It is triggered by the experience
of a juxtaposition (or what brain researchers called a ‘mismatch’ experience).
AEDP TERMS:
METAPROCESSING is ‘processing the process,’ in other words spending time
processing one’s experience of a positive shift, and the work that led to it, and
the new meanings it opens to view.
IFS TERMS:
Self Energy holds all the qualities of our true, adult self. It is the Self that
emerges when we are not overcome by one of our parts, but rather in a brain
state where our full emotional intelligence is online (what Daniel Siegel would
call neural integration)
Unblending is the act of stepping back from our parts and observing them in a
mindful way. When the ‘seat of our consciousness’ is being governed by a part
(for instance when we are ‘triggered’), we are blended with that part. When we
unblend, we have access to Self Energy.

STEP 1: Point of Focus

IDENTIFYING FOCUS / TARGET ISSUE
One of the first places CLARITY comes into play is around finding the point of
focus for the work. This would certainly be done during an intake, but also at
the beginning of sessions to orient toward the piece of work that will be
approached that day.
“What is it you would like me to help you with?”
Use any question you already use in order to learn from people why they
came to therapy. The thing to remember here is to be active in helping the
client find an internal focus. If they launch into complaints about other people,
for instance, gently return them to the task of finding an internal,
psychological problem they want help with.
“As we are beginning, I really want to spend some time with you figuring out
how we can best use our time, and what exactly it is you’d like my help with.
Let’s take a number of minutes here, because I want to be clear as to exactly
what you are wanting to work on. What kind of change you are looking for, or
what suffering you are experiencing that we need to understand and resolve.
Is that an okay place to start?”
“If our work ended up being incredibly helpful to you, what kind of change
would you experience?”
Or, at the beginning of a session (during ongoing work), you might ask:
“What is it you are wanting to focus on today?“
“Last week we were doing work around your difficulty telling people when you
disagree. Is there anything that would keep us from continuing with that work
today?“
“Take a moment as we get started just to let yourself ‘arrive.’ Then just check
inside and see what it is that you‘d most like to work on today.“

STEP 1: Point of Focus
WHEN FINDING FOCUS ISN’T EASY
People may need your help fully articulating a ‘psychological problem,’ (for
lack of a better word). While it is true that people suffer due to hard life
circumstances, most people have some place(s) inside that could use
healing work. And that is what we want to aim to clarify. So if they begin to
talk about a conflict with their partner, for instance, you’d certainly want to
start by offering empathic reflection around their suffering, but then soon
after ask questions like:
“So as we begin doing some work around this, I’d really like to explore with
you what it is you’d like to work on regarding this piece. I mean, it does
sound very stressful. But I’m also curious if there's any place inside where
you respond in a way that creates more difficulty for you, or you’d like to
change. Whether you self-attack, or feel overwhelmed, or shut-down, or get
defensive. You know? I’m curious how you find yourself responding to this
difficulty, and if there’s any place you're getting stuck that perhaps we could
work on?”
“Is there any part of your response to this all that you’d like to change, or be
different?”
“How do you find yourself responding when this happens? Do you feel you
are managing it well, or is there some place it takes you--either in your
emotions or behaviors--that you would like to see shift?”
“Wow, that aggressive tone from your boss sure sounds difficult to take. If
our work were to be successful, how would you want to feel or respond
differently than you are now?”
“When I think about suffering, I always visualize it as half coming from
things happening on the outside, and half coming from how we respond to
it...either in terms of behaviors, or emotional responses. Is there a response
you have to this situation that might be part of what makes those moments
so difficult?“

FLESHING OUT POINT OF FOCUS
When finding a focus for therapy, we need to understand what clients mean by
the words they use, because we use words fairly idiosyncratically. So what one
person means when they say ‘depressed,’ or ‘low self-esteem,’ could be far
different from what another person means. So you need to ask questions like:
“Can you describe in detail what you mean by ‘depressed’? What does this
look and feel like for you?”
Because we want to get as clear a picture as possible as to what the client is
wanting to change, we need to go past labels and general descriptions, and
hear from the client exactly how the issue plays out for them. Like getting an
‘operational definition’ with when/where/what.
For the ‘what’ you might simply ask for a description, or say:
“If I were to summarize back what I’ve heard, what would I need to say that
would let you know I understand the most important elements and have a clear
sense for what you are experiencing?”
Get totally clear on the details: “What kind of mistake does this seem to relate
to? Are all mistakes horrible, or just moral ones?”
For the ‘when/where’ (in terms of identifying triggers):
“I know you mentioned that this sinking feel (anxiety/obsessing) happens all
the time, but are there times it is even worse?” (Even when people experience
chronic concerns like low mood, they usually aren’t totally consistent. They
might be slightly worse during certain situations or times of the year, or around
certain people, or after certain triggers, etc.) “Are there certain people who this
plays out with more commonly or powerfully?” (or a certain situation / moment,
etc.)
For the ‘when/where’ you can also try to begin identifying possible historical
pieces: “Was there a particular time in your life this issue began?” Then, “can
you tell me about that time?” or “Does this experience feel familiar to you?”

CONTRACTING
Sometimes step 1 is super easy and just flows effortlessly into step 2, where
people naturally begin to turn inward and explore. However, sometimes people
need a little more time to be “Socialized to Therapy” (that’s a research term). It
can help to provide some explanation around what the process is all about.
There is a particular version of this step when the person doesn’t actually have
awareness that their pattern is causing them difficulty. They may be aware of
the unwanted ‘side-effects’ of their pattern, but don’t link it to the pattern itself.
For instance, they might come in complaining about conflict with their partner,
but not be aware that something they are doing is contributing to the problem.
Whew! So that’s a whole other step that is occasionally needed! I think the
bottom line in this case (where the ‘symptom’ is actually ‘ego-syntonic,’) is to
gently begin making the connection between the pattern and their suffering.
And then to respectfully explore the cost of that pattern. Once they see the
cost, they will be more motivated to take that pattern as a point of focus for
therapy.
For most people, however, the need to slow down is simply to make the
process feel safe, and a lot of that has to do with clarity...with the person
understanding a bit about what will be asked of them and why. That clarity
helps them fully ‘sign-up’ and feel empowered to collaborate.
In essence, we are wanting to regulate any shame or anxiety that might be
triggered by the idea of exploring inwardly. Clarity as to the process can help. It
can also help to normalize (explain why we ALL struggle).
In the next page, I’ll offer a few different versions of ‘introductions to the
process’. But I encourage you to write out at least 5 of your own. You might
want to craft one you can use with all new clients, and then a few others you
might draw from as needed. Try a few different styles as well (how you
describe process to an artist might be different than an engineer). We need to
be ready if a client asks “Why are we doing this?” or “How will this help?”

CONTRACTING (2)
Clients need to have some sense around why you are asking the kind of
questions you are asking. So come up with your own way to describe the
process. Some ideas are below.
“Would you like for us to explore what might be underneath this?”
Honestly this simply question is often all that is needed. The phrase
‘underneath this’ hints at the fact that there may be something underneath to
explore. And most people get that...
“Okay, so yes, that sounds like something really worth exploring...why you
never say no to anyone. So, if you are willing, let‘s really focus our exploration
right here, and just see what we can uncover about this.”
If they seem to need more, you could say things like:
“You know, often when we find ourself stuck in a pattern, there is some
unconscious belief that is driving it. You see, our brain evolved to just pick up
these imprints around how we should respond to things. And they are wired in
a way that we aren’t naturally aware of them. So when we want to change a
reaction we have, the first step is to slow down and see what kind of imprint--or
unconscious belief-- is driving that response in the first place. Of course as we
move along, we might bump into underlying emotions as well, and I’ll be here
to help you work through those. How does that all sound?”
Depending on how they respond you might continue with:
“So in our work, I’ll be continually asking you to slow down and notice or
explore an internal reaction. Sometimes I might ask you to check inside for an
emotional reaction, or to see what you feel. Sometimes I’ll be guiding you to
sense into what something means to you--not rationally, but on a gut level.
Honestly, this might feel strange at first. Cause most of us have never been
invited to deeply reflect and be mindful of ourself like this. But if you are open
to giving it a try, I’m am here to guide you.” Again, write down 5-10 versions
that capture what you believe therapy is really about. And then study the ones
you really like.

CONTACT WORK
Once you feel that you and the client have gotten on the same page around
what they are wanting to work on (including what the issue looks and feels
like and when / where it plays out), then you can shift into creating
compassionate contact with whatever might be underneath. This means we
are mindfully turning toward whatever comes up so it can teach us about
what the unconscious mind is holding in relation to this issue.
If we can imagine that clarifying the target issue is like finding the place in the
ground to dig, CONTACT is more about actually digging, ie slowing down
and inviting into awareness whatever hidden part of us or our experience is
ready to be seen or witness / explored.
Now, sometimes when we begin the digging, it’s like we hit a little cavern,
and the person just falls right into it! In other words, they become
overwhelmed because too much is coming at once. Helping them come out
of the cavern and regain a bit more distance is then important, and is still a
type of CONTACT work, because you can’t have meaningful contact with a
part of your experience if it is flooding you. That is why Contact is not only
about moving closer to the material, and more about finding the RIGHT
DISTANCE (basically same as IFS idea of remaining unblended). Knowing
how to deal with overwhelm is important, but I will outline that later in this
handout. So for now let’s simply explore how to DEEPEN contact through
asking questions that invite the client to turn toward their experience and
open their awareness inwardly.
To summarize, once the client has identified a particular point of focus, the
next step is to create a mindful internally-focused awareness such that the
part of them or their experience that they are wanting to explore can be
explored. I think of this as creating a ‘LISTENING SPACE.’ And it is truly a
sacred space, where there is room to simply witness what is real without
needing to judge or turn away.

WORKING WITH AN EXAMPLE
One of the best ways to begin making contact with the piece you are wanting to
explore is to ask for an example. You might say:
“I’d like you to think of an example where this played out. You could either pick a
recent example, or perhaps one that really captures your usual experience of
(berating yourself for a mistake). Does one come to mind?”
“Could you think of a time that this played out recently? Or maybe a typical
example?”
“Good. If you will, I’d love you just to walk me through that moment. You might
even tell it to me in the first person, like “I’m just walking in the door. And I see
that my wife isn’t home yet.” I’ll keep slowing you down so that we can really
explore what is happening internally and not miss anything.“
“I’d like you just to move through that moment in your mind, and notice the first
moment that the (sinking feeling) comes up.”
Then, “What is happening just before that moment?”
“If you can just rewind and kind of freeze frame that moment. What thoughts or
feelings are going through your mind?”
“Okay, so yes, there seems to be a lot that happens inside right there. Right then
when she turns away...yes...let’s hover right there.’ (or ‘linger there’, ‘or ‘stay
there’ or ‘get curious and try to see what we can discover here’).
“So as your brother is walking toward you, what are you noticing? Either in him or
you. What things are coming up as he approaches?”

WORKING WITH AN EXAMPLE (2)
Working within an example is incredibly helpful. It allows you to slow down,
rewind, get curious about what is/was happening internally (so you can then
move either in the direction of exploring COHERENCE or doing COMPLETION
work). Examples help the client come into genuine contact with their mind or
experience as it actually happens/happened, rather than just talking vaguely or
intellectually about it. Examples provide a portal back into their experience so
they can explore it.
Of course, sometimes the issue is active in the moment, or is playing out in the
room (like the client noticing difficulty being honest with the therapist and an
impulse to please them, or the client suddenly getting tense when asked what
they are feeling). It is therefore also an option to simply notice experience in
real-time, almost using the actual moment in the office as the ‘example.’ Then
you might say something like:
“Okay, thanks for sharing that. Great. So can we rewind to the moment you
noticed wanting to please me? If we can go back...maybe we can just slow that
down and see what was happening internally for you.”
“So when I asked how you felt, what happened inside? I thought I saw you
getting a bit tense about it?...Oh, you did? Okay, yeah, so let’s just slow down
with that moment and see what happens inside right there.”
Examples aren’t the only option to start deepening the work--in fact, in IFS they
use the 6 F’s (you can easily find these online). This is a more somatic
approach, where you simply begin to try to touch into and make contact with a
part. This is also great, but I do find using an example to be powerful and
reliable. So I would suggest trying it out.
In the next pages, I will list out a number of different kinds of ‘CONTACT
QUESTIONS,’ starting with slowing people down. Practicing them will give you
a good feeling for how to invite inner awareness of experience (rather than
asking questions that take the client into their heads).

GUIDING CLIENT TO SLOW DOWN
“Let’s slow down right here. Yeah (therapist takes long breath).”
“Let’s stay with this piece, and begin to explore what is here. Is that okay?”
“Let’s linger here.” OR “Let’s stay here together.” OR “Let’s hover here.”
“Can we pause here and just notice?”
“Hmmm...okay, let’s aim toward discovering what is underneath this. Does
that sound good to you?”
“So let’s pause here and see where we are. Hmm...yes...you were saying
XYZ, but we weren’t clear about (why that feeling emerged, etc.). Okay, yes.
So let’s just see if your mind is willing to show us something about that.”
“Is it feeling workable to continue with this exploration? Just slowing
noticing each piece as it comes?”
“Is it okay to slow down like this, where we are really just inviting each
piece to come up and gradually add to what we are able to be in touch with
here?”
“I really appreciate how you are willing to explore and stay curious with me
like this, where we just keep aiming our awareness toward the next piece
your mind is willing to reveal here about why XYZ feels so necessary...”
“Feels like what you just said was really important. Let’s just stay with that
for a second. Let’s let that, kind of, ‘breath’.”
“Wow, could you say that again?” (Or the therapist might repeat the words).
(alt: “I’d love you to say XYZ again, and then take a full breath.”)

GUIDING CLIENT TOWARD COMPASSIONATE
CURIOSITY (“SELF ENERGY”)
“Is is possible just to be curious about this together? Just to kind of offer your
mind some space to show us what this is all about?”
“How are you feeling toward this part of you (or this sadness, etc.)?“ (This is a
classic IFS question to check to see if there is enough ‘SELF ENERGY’ (or
what I’m calling ‘CONTACT’)).
“How are you relating to this, as you are describing it? Does it feel workable, or
like it’s too much? (alt: are you feeling pretty open and understanding toward
yourself here, or is there judgement coming up?).
If the client says that they are judging or scared of or angry toward a part of
their experience, then you would shift focus and work with this judging/scared
part first (whether you call it a part, or just a concern, etc). First you can simply
as it to ‘soften back,’ but if it can’t, then you would want to listen to that part’s
(or client’s) ‘concerns’ and address them all. This might look like making a plan
for how it will be safe to move forward without getting overwhelmed, etc., or
explaining the concept of coherence so they realize that any part of their mind
is simply trying to help them, and has learned to do exactly what it is doing for
a good reason, even if we don’t know that reason yet. Explaining the concept
of coherence (or ‘no bad parts’) can really help increase compassion.
IFS is particularly focused on making sure the client is in ‘Self Energy’ (rather
than being blended with a part that is judgmental/scared/angry toward the
target part). HOWEVER, while it might be true that you need enough ‘Self
Energy’ online for the work to proceed, I have found that you don’t really need
full self-energy. So I’m usually much less attentive to checking for full selfcompassion (unless this is something that we are purposefully working on). I
think you can move forward as long as the therapist is in Self energy the client
is reasonably grounded / regulated (and curious). (More on working with
dysregulation later).

WORKING WITH DEFENSES
While the therapist is always encouraging the client to get stay in contact
with their deeper mind, this is particularly true (and sometimes tricky)
when people are in active avoidance of their mind. As a general rule, I
wouldn’t right away ‘call this out,’ but rather simply try to use my presence
and guidance to steer them inward (in AEDP, they call this ‘melting the
defense’). We have to do our part to make it safe, as well as clear what we
are asking, etc.!! So being patient (persistent) and doing our part to create
safety is always the first step. But if this isn’t enough, then it may be that
the avoidance itself needs to become the first ‘point of focus’ for therapy.
If we are going to work with defenses, we need to first ‘make the implicit,
explicit’ (ie make the pattern conscious and see if they are something the
client actually wants to work on). It is super important not to come across
as shaming, and usually that comes down to tone of voice.
“I notice that when I ask you to check inside (or report on your experience,
etc.), something seems to block that (or something seems difficult about
that, or you tend to shift right back into the story, sort of skipping over
what is happening inside). Do you know what I mean?” If they do know
what you mean, then you can shift into contracting around whether they
would like to explore what is underneath that. Sometimes psycho-ed helps
(around why you are asking them to look inside at all).
Remember, not only might people be afraid of opening up, they also might
not know how BECAUSE NO ONE HAS EVER BEEN INTERESTED IN THEIR
INNER WORLD! Sometimes reflecting this can actually be a very loving way
to help people want to learn to track inward.
“I notice that when I ask questions about your inner experience, you
sometimes seem at a loss, or even say ’I don’t know.’ As if it’s a bit foreign
to describe your experience to someone, or even be asked about it. That
makes me wonder if perhaps you’ve never had someone be this deeply
curious about your inner world?”
A reflection like that is often very powerful, and can bring up emotion. So I
would definitely follow up with, “What’s it like to hear me say that?“

WORKING WITH DEFENSES: CLASSIC IFS TECHNIQUES
Different therapies have different ways of working with defenses, but all
of these revolve around ‘making the implicit, explicit.’ (ie bringing the
defense to awareness so it can be explored). And they all emphasize
non-judgement. In fact, it can be useful to not only accept the defense,
but also honor it. And appreciate the ways it has protected the client in
the past (ie do some ‘coherence work’ around the defense).
IFS does this piece very overtly (although it can be done in more subtle
ways that don’t necessarily include parts language). In fact a non-parts
language version might be something like:
“Okay, so let’s just learn about this then. We notice that when we step
closer to looking at XYZ, that you dissociated (or ‘that you tend to
change subjects or feel tense, etc.). So let’s not try to change that in any
way. Let’s just see if we can understand. Is that okay?”
While you don’t need to use parts language, the IFS method does give a
nice template. And that is this: when bumping up against defenses, we

“It seems like, while a part of you is willing to tell me about XYZ, another

WORKING WITH DEFENSES: PATIENCE and NON-AGENDA
One of the most powerful ways to create safety is through holding a stance
that we might call ‘non-agenda,’ where we purposefully let go of any need to
rush the work or have the client go deeper or do anything differently, etc. Yes,
ultimately we do want change (and relief and wellness) for our client. But we
aren’t urgent about making it happen on our time-line (ie right now!). Plus,
rushing and pushing doesn’t work (or if it works temporarily, it will backfire). So
instead we simply trust the process and focus on the work, rather than the
outcome.
I’ve been surprised how often simply giving ‘permission’ (or encouraging the
client to give themselves permission) to show up exactly as they are showing
up immediately shifts something. I used to have a client who didn’t like coming
to therapy and would express feeling grumpy to be there, and I’d always say,
“Okay, just let yourself really make space for that grumpiness. Let’s invite it to
be with us, if we can. It’s allowed here.” Of course then we’d look at what need
the grumpiness was expressing, etc., but the first step was just permission.
One thing I love about IFS is they always give permission for parts to show up
as they are showing up. So if a part is turned away and not trusting...then the
guidance might be, “Is it okay for you to have this part be turned away? Yes?
Good, then just let them know it’s okay for them to show up however they
want. There is no requirement that they trust you.” Ironically, expressing
understanding around why a part (or person) might NOT trust, and not pushing
that they get over that, is one of the fastest ways to build trust.
The main point here is that our systems don’t like being ‘managed,’ or being
related to with ‘agenda.’ We want to offer listening and attunement, not ‘fixing.’
And when that intention is clear, it often creates a lot of opening. This means
we have to work with our own ‘parts’ that do have agenda (that either need the
client to get better faster, or to like us, etc.). We all have those parts, and we
work with them the best we can. Similarly, we often need to work with client’s
parts that hold agenda (for instance that want the inner child to feel better right
away, etc.).

PREVENTING EMOTIONAL OVERWHELM
As I mentioned, there are two elements involved in CONTACT (which you can
relate to Daniel Siegel’s idea of ‘separation plus linkage’ (language which he
borrowed from complexity theory). First, we need to help people slow down,
sidestep defenses, and open up more inner awareness (ie we need enough
‘linkage’--or connection with what is underneath). But we also need to keep
things within the ‘window of tolerance,’ which means we also need enough
‘separation’ (observing distance). This balance is sometimes a dance, because
we are inviting more emotional information to flow upward into conscious
awareness, but also titrating the process enough to allow the experience to
feel safe and workable.
On the next page, I will show a diagram I often use with my students, called
the “Needs and Resources Channels.” The point of this diagram is to show that
everyone has two things inside of them. First, they have places inside that hold
unmetabolized (or even recognized) pain, unmet needs, unprocessed trauma
memories, unsolved problems, etc. Basically, places that deserve attention
and resources, so that work can be done to create a shift or change! Second,
they have resources inside them (and often on the outside, for instance from
others), that are not being recognized or tapped into. The goal is to slowly
become more and more aware of both. Why? Well, when we can bring our
deepest resources to our deepest pains or needs, that is when true healing
can happen.
This work on the ‘two channels’ is more applicable once we begin completion
work, because contacting both channels is what allows us to find the places
inside that actually need to ‘complete’ some processing, while holding enough
resource to do so. You see, completions happen (whether more superficial
completions, or deepening ones), when ‘resource meets need.’ In other words,
once our system is able to look at our true pain / emotions / needs in a
resourced manner, it is simply natural for things to begin to move toward
healing / processing / problem solving / insights (ie ‘completion’). And once
we’ve seen this happen on one level, it gives us courage to go more deeply.

Regulating Overwhelmed States
The ‘two channels’ diagram tracks a way of working that is aimed at
avoiding emotional overwhelm. However, overwhelm (what IFS people
would call ‘blending with an exile’) does happen--even when we do
everything right! In fact, sometimes people come in already overwhelmed!
So it is important to learn to work with dysregulated states, and actively
help clients regain their bearings.
There are so many options when working with emotion regulation,
including using what people typically think of as ‘resources’ (like deep
breathing, self-care, nature, relaxation, self-soothing, somatic techniques,
etc.). Depending on what kind of work you like to do, you may enjoy
building up some of these offerings (like if you do somatic work, you will no
doubt learn some somatic ways of regulating one’s nervous system, etc.).
You can also dive into Deb Dana’s work (drawing on polyvagal theory), if
you work with people with severe trauma and really need a little ‘extra’
around this piece. And of course, all therapists should continually work on
their ability to be warm and create safety interpersonally through their
capacity for attunement!
However, my two main ‘go-to’s when it comes to emotion regulation are
less about simply ‘soothing’ someone, and more around coming into
awareness that what they are experiencing is a memory, rather than just
pure emotion. In other words, much of the time when people are
dysregulated, it is because they are having an ‘emotional flashback,’ where
old pain is being remembered...but, because the pain / experience itself
was never processed (or never integrated by the hippocampus, as is
common with trauma)...it feels like it is happening NOW. In other words, it
comes up more like implicit memory than explicit memory. And because
implicit memory feel like ‘this is just the nature of reality,’ versus ‘this is a
memory of the past I am remembering,’ the memory can emerge more like
a ‘flashback’. Unlike a traditional full-on flashback (where the person
hallucinates the actual scene), this is more like a somatic version, where
they relive the experience in their bodies / emotions / what it ‘felt like’ to
be there.

Regulating Overwhelmed States (2)
When people (somatically / emotionally) relive their old pain in this way, they
don’t know they are having a memory. So it feels current, which both makes it
more intense and alarming, but also more confusing (like, “Why am I reacting
in this way?!!”). So the aim here is to help them realize that what they are
experiencing is memory-based, and thereby both get some distance from it,
while realizing they aren’t crazy, nothing horrible is necessarily happening
NOW, etc.
I therefore simply start by explaining the science of emotional flashbacks (very
briefly). I might say: “You know, Devon, would you mind if I shared something
that might help? Well, my sense is that some of what you are feeling right now
might actually be what I call an ‘emotional flashback’. In other words, the
current situation is reminding you of how bad it felt back then (when your mom
would attack you), and an actual memory got triggered. But the thing is, really
painful memories are stored in the brain differently. Because of stress, the
brain often fails to put a ‘time-stamp’ on the memory, which means that when
we remember it, it doesn’t feel like remembering, it feels like reliving. And all
the old feelings just flood through our body, as if the old situation is happening
right now. You see what I mean? Yeah. You might have heard of how
flashbacks can bring back a whole scene so it’s like the person is back
there...this is like that, but more in the body or emotions.” After checking
whether this makes sense, I might add, “The good news is that we can help
the brain out here. We can give it the message, ‘This is a memory. It isn’t
current.’ And often simply knowing that can be very orienting and soothing.”
Okay, so once a general description of the science is laid-out, there are two
directions you can go. One is from coherence therapy and is called the “I’m in
memory exercise,” and other is from IFS and is a particular type of
negotiation with exiles to assist in ‘unblending.’ I’ll describe both on the next
two pages.

“I’m in Memory Exercise”
The “I’m in Memory Exercise” comes out of coherence therapy (and is outlined
in more depth in the book “Unlocking the Emotional Brain”), and is designed for
flooded states that relate to a particular trigger.
When doing the “I’m in Memory Exercise,” you would first want to explain the
science / reasoning behind the exercise, much like I did on the last page (or
read Bruce Ecker’s way of describing it in the above-mentioned book). Then you
might say something like:
“Alright, Kat, if you will, put a hand over your heart. And I’m going to say some
statements and I want you to repeat after me. Ready? Okay. ‘When my wife is
late getting home, and I can’t reach her (pause while they repeat), it reminds me
of all those times when mom would leave me with my cold dad, and I didn’t
know when she’d be back, and I felt so alone and forsaken’. Great. Okay, now
just pat your heart gently and say: ‘it felt so horrible back then, and this situation
is reminding me of it’. Great, now let’s do it one more time, and I’ll lead you
again.“
When doing the “I’m in Memory Exercise,” the client is offered the statement on
a card (or email) and given the homework to read it to themselves at any
moment of the particular trigger causing intense distress. Although here I’m
outlining this exercise for the sake of emotion regulation (which has value itself),
in truth this exercise has the potential to also be a moment of memory
reconsolidation. Why? Because the network that holds the memory of the pain
‘believes’ the pain (from the past) is happening now. As mentioned, because
traumatic memories are more likely to be held unconsciously or implicitly, they
fail to get updated with current reality, and additionally (when triggered) actively
predict that the danger or difficultly is current. Therefore, to feel the activation,
while witnessing that it is just a memory, has the potential to serve as a
mismatch. It might need to be repeated a number of moments, but each time is
likely to lessen the activation ongoingly, through actually changing the
‘prediction’ held by that neural network.

“Unblending from Exiles”
Now let’s look at how overwhelm is handled in IFS work. Their approach is
similar to the “I’m in Memory Exercise,” (and many trauma therapy techniques,
in fact), in that the point is to get some ‘observing’ distance from the
part/memory that is being activated. Trauma therapists call this ‘differentiation
work.’ While there are many ways to do it, it is very naturally accomplished
during parts work through the process of ‘unblending’ (which means to bring an
observing awareness toward a part in a mindful way). And while it is possible
to have a protector part who has a lot of emotional activation, usually when
someone is overwhelmed it is because they are ‘blended’ with an exile. Really
this is the same idea as having an emotional flashback, just described through
‘parts language’, where the exile has blended with the Self (ie taken over the
seat of consciousness), and the goal is to help it to understand that it would be
in its best interests to pull back a bit (unblend).
Because IFS is a very relational model (where we relate to our inner processes
as if they are people), here’s how what they might say to a client: “What I’m
thinking is that a very young/hurt part of you has filled up your awareness, and
we actually need to ask it to step back a bit. But here’s the thing: often these
parts flood us because they are so sick of being stuffed in the closet and need
us to finally see the pain they carry. So we will need to explain to him/her/them
that we want them to give you a bit more breathing room. But NOT so that we
can push them away again, but rather so that you have just enough room to
actually be with them. You see, when this young part floods you, your adult self
goes away. And then the young one is simply alone again. Which is not what
they want. So if we can gently explain that to this part, it may be willing to step
back just a bit, and pull some of its big feelings back into its own body, so that
you can stay fully present to listen to and support it. I’d be happy to speak
directly to this part too, if that feels better. I believe we can work as a team on
this, and help this part get its needs met and be heard--without having to
overwhelm you.” Of course, if the part is willing to pull back, then you’d have to
keep your side of the promise of then fully listening to it.

Additional Thoughts:
The previous two exercises are a bit more around a particular (old) pain or
memory being reactivated (which is probably usually at play). But there is
also the possibility of ANY emotion feeling overwhelming due to a schema
that emotions are dangerous. So this would be a more general fear of
emotion or pain, versus fear of reliving a particular painful experience they
had historically. I don’t think this is a clear distinction (probably both are
usually at play to one degree or another), but if you notice that any
emotional accessing is triggering for the person, you can wonder if there’s
a general phobia of (usually negative) emotion(s).
Obviously titration and co-regulation (and all the pieces we explored in the
‘two channels’ discussion) will be very applicable here. It can be helpful to
name the fear of emotion (and why it might be coherent given their
history), or even do coherence work around this (through crafting a schema
statement, as outlined later...in fact, the example schema statement I give
on pg. 50 is around fear of emotion). But whether you craft a whole
statement, or just briefly explore how they seem to have anxiety/shame
around emotions, the eventual place you will want to go is to get their
permission to purposefully ‘move toward’ the emotion “in small enough
steps” (as Bruce Ecker would say). You can do this in any way that feels
natural, but this can begin to look like AEDP work.
One more offering: systems centered therapy (SCT), has some useful
thoughts on helping people stay grounded. One thing they’ve noticed is
that people can feel overwhelmed when there is more than one emotion
and they haven’t been separated and named. They have a ‘facts and
feelings’ technique, where they have the person name the facts (note: “He
abandoned me,” isn’t a fact; “He was 30 minutes late” is a fact), and then
find the feeling(s) that goes with each fact. Additionally, when people seem
to be stuck in a schema that is equating past to present, then it can be
helpful to name 10 differences between the past / present (or between my
father and husband, for instance). I have found this can be incredibly
regulating, and a helpful step (again, from SCT).

The previous pages explored the pieces of work I call “Contact” and “Clarity,”
and while I put them first because they are foundational requirements that
make inner exploration possible, they are truly elements that continue through
all of the stages of work. In other words, you will always be supporting
CONTACT (mindful witnessing / unblending), and CLARITY (focusing on one
piece or part at a time). Keeping those in place is what allows for Discovery
work to unfold.
As you are doing Discovery work, there are two equally valid pieces worth
exploring: either emotions (which then can move into COMPLETION work), or
emotional learnings (schemas, which is what we might call COHERENCE
work). Likely you will do some of both, and that work can happen in either
order, or go back and forth, as the work progresses. I will say, I tend to lean
into COHERENCE work first, because if I can get a really clear view of the
emotional learning, I will know more exactly what kind of COMPLETION (if
any) might be needed (in order to serve as a disconfirmation).
AEDP therapists, on the other hand, lean right into COMPLETIONS
(discovering emotions and ‘working them through’). These therapists (and
others) put emotional processing (completion) at the center, and assume
‘understanding’ (coherence) will emerge as needed. Conversely, Coherence
therapists put Coherence work at the center, and assume that emotional
processing will emerge as needed. There’s really no right or wrong, and you
can decide what approach suites you or the client.

STEP 3: COHERENCE WORK
Coherence work is all about discovering what emotional learnings (schemas,
imprints, unconscious expectations) make sense of the client’s suffering (or
‘symptom’). This work looks like crafting questions (or visualizations, etc.)
aimed at helping reveal the ‘urgent purpose’ or schema underneath the
symptom.
Every schema has two parts:
1) the knowledge of a suffering or problem, and
2) the knowledge of how to avoid that suffering or problem.
The aim is ultimately to create a ‘juxtaposition’ that works to ‘disconfirm’ either
part of the emotional knowing (more on that later).
But before we can move into searching for disconfirmations, we must first
discover and map out the schema...both in terms of what problem or suffering
it knows about, and ways (if any) it has learned to avoid it. (I say ‘if any’
because sometimes schemas know that there is some danger, and NO
solution or means of avoiding it. Often disconfirmations here relate to realizing
they DO have solutions / resources now they didn’t have when young).
Any time you are doing Coherence work, you are asking the deeper mind what
it knows (predicts, expects, anticipates, fears, etc.). In IFS work, this looks like
asking a protector part what it fears would happen if it didn’t do its job (or even
how an exile made sense of things, perhaps assuming it was their fault, for
instance). Any time you are asking about what was learned, you are doing
coherence work, and have a chance to outline a schema.
For a detailed analysis of how Coherence Therapy approaches this work, I
would suggest purchasing the “Coherence Therapy Manual” on Amazon. But
you can also use IFS techniques. I’ll just try to outline some of the key
techniques from both these theories on the next pages.

STEP 3: Coherence Work

COHERENCE THERAPY’S DISCOVERY PROCESS
Doing discovery work around coherence does not need to be technique-heavy.
Simply slowing down, inviting the client to look inward for what they know
about this (with a tone of voice that invites listening for new awareness, rather
than saying what they already know). You might say:
“Hmmm. So right here. I wonder if somewhere inside you know something
about this...”
Or “Just check inside. See what you know about this...” “Let’s slow down here,
because I wonder if you know something about being left behind (or whatever
the trigger is).”
This simple, conversational approach is called ‘empathic eliciting,’ where you
ask very specifically what the client predicts or what danger or problem they
anticipate. Another example of it would be:
“So you find yourself in that moment really staying quiet. Yes, I see. So I guess
that leads me to wonder whether some part of your previous experience taught
you that it is dangerous in some way, or not a good idea, to voice your
concerns. Some experience where it didn‘t go so well. Does anything come to
mind about this?“
If the client names a particular fear (like, “If I‘m not perfect, I‘ll be left behind,”),
then you might ask, “What‘s the worst or strongest you‘ve ever experienced of
that...of being left behind?” (Pretty heavy-hitting question, so I would ask it as
part of inviting us to slow down, showing non-verbally that I‘m right there with
them).
This kind of coherence work has a conversational, exploratory tone as we try
to figure out how things deeply make sense. But it isn’t heady. We want it to be
‘bottom-up’, so we slow down, encourage answers to come from within, stay
curious, give space for new awareness to emerge, etc.
There are some techniques you can use, and those will be outlined next.

OVERT STATEMENTS
One of the backbone ‘techniques’ of Coherence therapy is to use language to
try to capture or represent the emotional truths or expectations held by the
schema. While these knowings aren’t held verbally, when the subcortical brain
hears words that closely match what it knows, it does resonate. And it can
supply it’s own language as well. This is called ‘limbic language,’ and it tends
to be short, emotionally vivid, personal, and direct. Like “I can’t bear to be
wrong on this!” “If she leaves, no one will love me again!”
So when doing coherence discovery work, it is important to be writing down
what you hear the client say, particularly anything that seems like an emotional
learning. That way you can ask for more clarification, or even pull the threads
together and reflect them back. You might, for instance, say:
“Would you be willing to try saying something out loud? Just as an
experiment? To see if it feels true or not as you say it.” (Then offer statement
as you understand it, with full permission for them to change any part that
doesn‘t feel true as they say it).
OR,
“Okay. So is it like this?: ‘If I get even the slightest hint of bad news, I must run
through all the worst case scenarios in my mind. I can’t bare to be caught off
guard and unprepared. I must never let myself be surprised by bad things, so I
will imagine them first, so they can’t shock me.’ Is that getting close to it?” (Of
course, if such a statement did resonate, we would then need to explore what
they know about ‘being shocked’ as such a terrible thing that they can’t handle.
So the work is step by step, like reverse engineering.)
Crafting the statement is a collaborative process, and is not our chance to
make an interpretation, even though we may tentatively offer some words
(mainly drawing on words they already used).

OVERT STATEMENTS (2)
Often one way to begin to get the relevant material for the overt-statement, is to
use a technique called ‘SENTENCE COMPLETION‘. In it, you simply ask the
client to ‘allow your mind to fill in the rest of the sentence, without pre-thinking
what should come.’ Then you begin with the thing the client is doing that is part of
their pattern, and leave the rest of the sentence (around why it is urgent or
protective) blank.
You might ask:
“So I’ll start a sentence, and I want you to just let your mind complete the
sentence. In fact, just keep repeating it about 10 times, without thinking too much.
Okay, here’s the sentence: ‘I must never let anyone see me being weak, because
if I do...’”
Another powerful way to use overt statements is to direct the statement toward
the person involved with the learning. (“Can you picture your mom before you and
just tell her candidly, “Mom, you don‘t want to hear about my sadness.”) Some
sensitivity is needed here, because it can be difficult to do, particularly if they are
likely to imagine the person coming back with an abusive remark (as they would
in real life).
If it seem workable, you can ask the client to speak:
1) in the present-tense (all overt statement should be in the present tense!)
2) to the emotionally relevant person(s) (if applicable)
3) while clearly visualizing them (possibly in an emotionally relevant scene)

SYMPTOM DEPRIVATION
If you want to give extra ‘juice’ to a sentence completion (or just to the
Discovery process in general), it can be helpful to first really bring online the
network that ‘knows’ to do exactly as it is doing (ie to launch the symptom).
One way to do this is through what is called a ‘symptom deprivation’. In this
technique, you ask the client to imagine being without their symptom (or doing
something that is not in line with their pattern), and allow this to stimulate a
sense of discomfort for them.
In other words, you walk them through a very vivid moment where the
symptom usually shows up, and imagine it NOT showing up. The following
example is inspired by one I once heard Bruce Ecker give:
“Okay, so there you are. You just came home. And you walk to the kitchen and
pick up the heavy glass you use for liquor. You put a few pieces of ice in the
glass. But then, for whatever reason, you just put the glass down and walk
away. You don’t take a drink at all (pause). Just take a moment to imagine
that. Hmm...so what are you noticing inside?”
The idea is that to imagine being without one’s ‘symptom’ (even ones we say
we hate), naturally brings on some anxiety or discomfort. When/if it does, you
can then ask the discomfort to finish the sentence... “I must go ahead and have
that drink, because if I don’t...”
The symptom deprivation technique can be very useful, but it does take some
practice to make it effective. You could reference the coherence therapy
manual for more info, or even check out a free Youtube by Niall Geoghegan
called “Coherence Therapy: How to Create a Symptom Deprivation.” While
coherence therapy isn’t the only therapy that at times explores the coherence
of a symptom, it certainly does so with the most precision and depth. If you
would like to learn about this wonderful form of therapy, please reference my
favorite psychology book of all time: “Unlocking the Emotional Brain:
Memory Reconsolidation and the Psychotherapy of Transformational Change,”
by Ecker, Ticic, and Hulley.

PARTS WORK
What I just described are some techniques that come out of coherence
therapy. But you could certainly use parts work to do coherence work. In the
classic IFS protocol, you might ask a proctor part:
“What are you afraid would happen if you didn’t XYZ (criticise Beth)”? (Beth
being the client’s name)
This is not very different than the idea of sentence completion. It’s just that it
can be useful to use parts language (either as part of a whole process where
you unblend and really try to get in touch with a ‘part’, or just as a simply
reference to parts, like, ‘okay, so a part of you seems really driven to do XYZ,”
or “a part of you is alarmed by that idea.”)
There really isn’t any rule as to whether to do coherence work through parts or
not. But here are some things to consider:
For some people, parts language speeds things up. They get it, and they like it.
For others, it actually slows things down. They become preoccupied with the
fact that they can’t quite picture the part, or aren’t sure if it’s still ‘there,’ etc. But
while parts tracking can be difficult for some folks (and isn’t strictly necessary
to the process), it can be particularly effective in these cases:
1) for clients who need help relating to their own inner world with clarity (like
seem confused by their conflicted feelings, etc.),
2) for clients who are vulnerable to feeling shame around their issue(s)
(although explaining the concept of Coherence can work as well here),
3) for clients who might easily become overwhelmed (and could benefit from
unblending).
For people who feel pretty grounded and curious, etc., (perhaps you could say
already have enough ‘Self energy’ online), then I don’t always bother with
unblending, and might say, “okay, you learned something about this,” or, “It
feels true that...” instead of ‘a part of you believes...’

MOMENT-BY-MOMENT TRACKING
As you move through questions around what the client is feeling, you want to
do moment-by-moment tracking. AEDP’s ‘moment-by-moment tracking,’ is
similar to my ‘two channels,’ described previously. In other words, as we are
slowly deepening into more awareness of the Needs (Pain, Losses, Feelings,
Unprocessed Memories) underneath, we also are deepening into new and
deeper levels of resource.
So how does ‘moment-by-moment’ tracking support this dance (or
‘pendulation’)? Well, for one, the simple act of noticing what is happening now
(being mindful) both supports awareness of the parts inside that need
awareness (the “Needs”), while at the same time allowing a sense of resource
(because the simple act of paying mindful attention is very resourcing). That is
why awareness is so powerful. Because it plays ‘double duty,’ through bringing
our full intelligence online, so that we can more deeply see BOTH the pain we
hold inside more clearly, while also accessing the resource (ie compassion /
mental space / perspective) more clearly.
So you can think of ‘moment-by-moment tracking,’ as a mindfulness
practice...one that’s ‘moment-by-moment’ because the act of being mindful
allows a deepening in both channels, which means things are shifting inside!
And we want to follow those shifts. Why? Because we will do slightly different
things depending on where the client is. If they are hitting up against emotion
(“Need”), then we encourage them to stay there (and ask if it is okay to do so).
If the material is causing anxiety or shame, then we can help move back
toward the Resource Channel. And if the client is noticing their resources /
capacity to effectively navigate inwardly (as in: “Oh, that actually feels soothing
to say that out loud” or “I feel compassion for myself”), then we can encourage
the client to mindfully notice that (which is called metaprocessing...more on
that later!). Or if they retract from inner awareness (through defenses), that can
be addressed. (If you know the ‘triangle of conflict,’ this will all sound familiar).
In short: mindfulness helps the client enter a resourced state AND allows them
to accurately report on what is happening, so we know how to help.

Co-Regulation (ala AEDP)
The “moment-by-moment tracking’ (just described) is the cornerstone of a
larger frame for using the relationship between client and therapist as a means
for deepening into both the resource/needs channel so that emotions can be
‘worked through’ (AEDP term for ‘completion’).
In AEDP, the therapist not only helps the client feel their own internal
resources, but also encourages them to notice the resource available through
the relational dance itself (so not only within, but between). This is done
through something called ‘co-regulation’. Again, the main key to co-regulation
is ‘moment-by-moment tracking’ (where we are following exactly ‘where’ the
client is, so we know what kind of help to offer). This is related to the idea of
‘attunement.’
Below are some additional tips from AEDP. These are core elements used
when helping people access their emotions and ‘work them through.’
1) Genuine, compassionate contact (between therapist and client, or self-topart): “I’m right here.” “Can you feel me here with you?”
2) Moment-by-moment tracking “And what’s happening now?”
3) Regulating shame/anxiety, whether interpersonally (ie through therapist’s
attunement / compassion), intrapersonally (ie through parts work / selfcompassion), through mindful (somatic) awareness (noticing sensation of
anxiety w/o judgement), or through tracking coherence (exploring emotional
‘learnings’ under shame/anxiety can be soothing).
4) Continual invitation toward emotion...patient and persistent guidance to
notice and stay with. “Let’s just linger here.” “Can you make space for the
sadness?”
5) Asking permission. Key question: “Is it okay to be with this?” If yes, then,
“Great, then just let it move through. This is really important...”. Asking for
permission is very regulating.

INNER CHILD WORK (ala IFS)
While pure emotional processing is a common completion that people need,
there are other (more imaginal) ways of facilitating completions that never got
to happen (from the past). One, of course, is through parts work!! There are
many systems that use parts in different ways, but we will stick with good old
IFS :)
In IFS, once you have done the (Coherence) work around exploring what
protector parts learned to fear, you can simply ask the ‘protector’: “who inside
are you protecting?” Then you can ask them if they are willing to soften back
and allow contact with that (usually young) part. There is a lot of guidance IFS
gives around this process (beyond what I will outline--and I encourage you to
learn more about the official IFS steps if you like parts work), but one key they
often use (when parts are scared to soften back), is to ask if they’d be willing to
soften back as long as the young part first agrees to not flood “Theo.” This
negotiation is pretty beautiful, where there is a conversation with the ‘exile’
ahead of time, explaining that, while you understand why they would flood
(because they need to be heard!), it unfortunately simply leaves them once
again alone because the adult self is swept offline. You can talk to young parts
and very lovingly explain that you need them to hold their emotion a bit
separate, so that “Theo” can stay present and finally be fully available to hear
them.
Obviously it can be very useful to learn the official IFS steps. But the bottom
line is that once you have contact with an exile, it is time to build a relationship.
And what is the master key to building a strong relationship, built on trust?
Listening. Listening is everything. This is not about ‘cheering up’ the part, or
rushing to make them feel better. Yes, one of the first questions to ask is
whether the part can feel the adult Self present. But if they do, and just don’t
want to connect...that’s okay! And even just giving permission for the
young/hurt part to show up exactly as it wants to can be powerful. This is a
version of ‘undoing aloneness,’ where the goal is to be curious about the hurt
part’s experience (or even mistrust), rather than immediately change it.

INNER CHILD WORK (2)
Inner child work (ie parts work) is an alternate way to move through the ‘arc of
completion.’ Below I will review how the steps of the arc are accomplished
through parts work:
Step 1: Contacting Resource.
When the adult Self is finally available (through unblending with protector parts),
the system now has the resource of an adult intelligence (compassion/wisdom,
etc.). This allows the system to open to new work and processing. Again, think
of this as ‘undoing aloneness.’
Step 2: Processing
Processing is all about the child (or exile) informing an adult about what is
happening. That way they can work together to find new meaning around it.
This is accomplished through the exile saying everything they experienced
(again, with the Self at first simply listening). That might range from what
happened (‘mom left!’), to how painful it was (‘I felt so scared!’), to what sense
they made of it (‘It was my fault!’). While the first step is for the Self to listen,
after listening there will likely be truths they can share with the child (“It wasn’t
your fault!” “I am grown and can protect you!” “You are valuable and innocent.”)
(Disonfirmations).
Step 3: Grief
Grief is about having the resources to begin bearing to metabolize and integrate
the hard experience or realities. We can’t grieve without a sense of resource /
connection / comfort. So once the adult self is there and has begun listening,
the whole system can finally begin to grieve. This might look like the child crying
on the Self’s (client’s) lap, etc.
Step 4: Action
Sometimes there are actions that seem clear once processing has happened.
So moving into action is often the final step of completion. In IFS, they often ask
the client to ask the part whether there is anything they want to do, or have the
Self (“Karen”) do. Often this is around fighting back, setting a boundary,
protecting the child, and finally...taking child to safe place (a tree house, the
client’s heart, the present moment, etc), where they can live forever after. (In
IFS there is also an ‘unburdening process,’ which I won’t review here).

EMPOWERED REENACTMENT
So far we have discussed ‘completions’ in relation to emotions that lean a bit
more ‘interpersonal’ (where the resource is likely to be relational, and lead into
discussions / exploration of needs and problem solving, etc.). The ‘completion arc’
looks a bit different around fear (at least when triggered by immediate physical
danger). Then the ‘resource’ becomes the body. And the ‘processing’ phase is
truncated down to a only a few seconds of orienting (checking out the danger,
looking for an exit, all while staying physically very still). Then right into action
(think ‘fight or flight’).
I think there are interesting parallels between attachment trauma (which is more
around interpersonal resource not be available) and physical traumas (which is
often about the body not being available--perhaps do to a stuck freeze response-or simply feeling insufficient to protect one’s self). Whereas the former leads to
schemas like “No one is there for me,” or “Sadness is a lonely and overwhelming
feeling, without resolution!” the latter leads to schemas around “I can’t protect
myself! So I’m in danger!” or “Fear is a stuck and overwhelming feeling, without
resolution!”
In both cases, the disconfirmation becomes a new experience of noticing and
allowing inner feeling/activation, and then supporting the process of resource
coming online, which allows for completion. If this sounds interesting (or you
enjoy somatic approaches), I would recommend training in either Somatic
Experiencing, or Sensorimotor Psychotherapy. They both specialize the helping
people notice their fear ‘activations,’ while going slowly enough to stay resourced.
This allows freeze to move into completion of ‘self-protective responses.’ This
provides a huge disconfirmations for the schemas that are driving the trauma
symptoms.
If you don’t want full training in somatic work, but still want to address schemas
picked up by physical traumas, I would suggest reading the section in “Unlocking
the Emotional Brain,” that outlines a version of this called ‘empowered
reenactment.’ The basis of this technique is to simply have the person imagine
the scene again, while then imagining themselves doing whatever is necessary to
protect themselves / fight back / run away, etc. This is an imaginal way of creating
the same kind of disconfirmation (of “I’m powerless”) through creating a new
experience (in this case through imagery) of effective self-protection.

WORKING WITH ANGER
So how does the previous page relate to working with anger, given that anger can be
about self-protection? While my sense is that anger is somewhere in between the
two ‘versions’ mentioned previously, I believe anger is ultimately an interpersonal
emotion (ie focused on solving interpersonal problems through interpersonal
conversations, boundaries, etc.). So whereas fear is about fight/flight for physical
safety, anger is more about getting another person’s attention with the main aim of
being heard. Why do we need to be heard? Because we deeply want to influence
another person’s actions--whether through talking to them, or (if they won’t listen),
yelling, pushing, physical controlling /punishing. The goal is to make them stop! (or
start).
While it usually isn’t productive to ‘act on’ unprocessed anger, it is a very healthy
emotion. Like all emotions, it gives information (‘pay attention to this!’) as well as
motivation and energy to act. The problem is, most people have a phobia of anger.
Either they saw a parent mishandle their own anger (like in the case of a parent who
rages) or repress their own anger (if parents never showed anger, that also teaches
something). Or the client was shamed / punished for their anger. So that is why we
need to actively make space for completions around anger in session. This can start
off with coherence work if you’d like (exploring messages they picked up from family
or culture about anger), or could move straight into moment-by-moment tracking
aimed at processing anger through to ‘completion’.
When processing anger, you ask all the ‘experiential questions,’ (“what were you
feeling toward her in that moment?,” “Where does that anger live in your body?” “Is it
okay to be with this and give some space for the anger to be felt?” Additional
questions might be: “What does the anger want to say to them?” or even “What does
the anger want to do? Right there, in your body. What would you do if you didn’t
have to hold back at all? I don’t mean what you should actually do in real life, but just
in imagination, what would you want to do to make them stop?” Then you can
support the client in imagining a physical response. Yes, these impulses can be
violent. Often we have deep rage about ways we were abused and couldn’t fight
back. So it can be incredibly relieving / empowering to let that finally move through in
a safe, grounded way. Central to most ‘anger processing’ is imagining aggressive
physical actions the anger ‘wants’ to do. If that sounds too much, imagining speaking
words (“Back the fuck off!”) can be powerful too.

People can have powerful experiences, or new ‘aha’s’, that then fall back into

which you work to craft language around during session, but the card holds
whatever was learned (not always a whole schema). Then you (together, if you
have time), write out the clearest version of this statement, with the homework
being to review it every other day for the next week (without trying to argue
with it).
Sometimes simply sitting with their ‘card’ creates spontaneous juxtapositions
for folks. But the main purpose is to facilitate integration, so that what was
wired unconsciously is now held consciously. This helps with therapeutic
focus, so that the next session can start with a check-in: ‘What was it like to
stay in contact with the contents of the card?’
I got permission (from client) to offer his schema as an example, developed
during 2nd session:
"Dad, I learned from you that for a boy to be sad and cry is weak and
pathetic. I believed you because to not believe you would put me at risk of
behaving in ways that you couldn't stand and wouldn't love. So I had to
toughen up! Not only to make you love me, but to avoid that horrible moment
of punishment where you made me feel so pathetic and weak for crying.
And it isn't just you, dad. Everyone secretly believes that it is pathetic for a
man to cry. So I must, at all costs, not let myself even go near those tears. I
must not even feel sad, even if that means I'm less connected to my joy. While
I hate feeling disconnected from my full, true self, it is better than being weak,
and everyone knowing that I'm weak.”
IFS has their own version of integration work, equally simple. The homework in
IFS is to simply ask the person to promise their part (if they feel they will follow
through) to check-in with them (particularly if it’s an exile) every day to renew
the connection, and see how they are feeling.

AEDP’S METAPROCESSING
Whereas the above ‘integration phase’ is used upon completion of a piece of
coherence work, if you have finished a piece of completion work, then you
would next want to ‘metaprocess.’
Metatherapeutic processing, or ‘metaprocessing’ for short, comes out of
AEDP. Metaprocessing is an opportunity to slow down after an important piece
of work and process it (hens the word ‘meta’ because you are ‘processing the
process’). When people finally move through a whole successful completion
(particularly around core wounds, but even just around feelings about current
life), it is a powerful experience. After core emotions are processed, people
almost invariable experience what are called ‘transformational affects,’ such as
relief, joy, peace, gratitude, strength, clarity, etc. Sometimes they feel another
round of grief for only now having this experience. In Fosha’s mind, these
emotions are important to explore as well.
To use our language, transformational affects should have their own
‘completion’. After all, emotions are all around ‘hey, process this! It is important
and deserves attention!’ and even these positive emotions have something
they need to process, which is, “Wow, what just happened? Why was that so
great? How did we get here? My goodness...I think I see something new about
what we just explored...I really see how my parents were trying their best...etc,
etc.” Why the last insight? Well, once we are freed up from the grip of the
negative emotion, and are finally bringing our adult insight into the whole thing,
we are likely to see new things that we never saw before, or have a new
perspective that we couldn’t find when we were either fully immersed in the
distress (or actively avoiding it).
In AEDP, they call this ‘autobiographical reconstruction,’ but it basically just
means that if you take the chance (after a piece of work) to linger, explore how
good it feels, and look again at what was being discussed, it is likely you will
see something new. Not only about your story, but, very centrally, about
whether it is safe to feel, and be seen by another.

So, how long to continue metaprocessing? Really we can stay with it until the
client has no more to add. This might just be five minutes, but often is fifteen or
more (I’ve spent whole sessions metaprocessing!). So don’t worry if it is feeling
long (“Um, I think I’m supposed to be helping them with their problems?”),

disconfirmations). My friend Juliane Taylor Shore love reciting poetry to help

STEP 5: TRANSFORMATION
THROUGH JUXTAPOSITION
Once you have done either a successful piece of completion or coherence
work, the next step is to aim toward the creation of what (in CT) is called a
‘juxtaposition experience.’ There are two things needed for this experience: 1)
activation of the original prediction / knowing / schema and 2) awareness of
information that subjectively, but decisively, contradicts it.
Now, much of the time this ‘disconfirmation’ will naturally have emerged during
STEP 4. Why? Well, in coherence work, as soon as the schema is clearly
stated, if the person already has access to disconfirming knowledge, it will
likely pop up. What about during metaprocessing? There too, just in reverse!
In other words, metaprocessing is all about sitting and witnessing the
disconfirming evidence first, but doing so will likely bring the original schema
into awareness. Although AEDP therapists don’t map out this schema ahead,
often when in metaprocessing, people will naturally say, “Hey, this is not what I
thought would happen if I allowed myself to feel my sadness (or anger)!” They
may have other spontaneous juxtapositions as well (like I mentioned can
happen when they look back at whatever painful life experience they just
processed from a newly centered, empowered position). Of course, if you are
doing emotional processing, and the person lands in a more expansive or atease place, you can contribute to the juxtaposition by asking, “Is this what you
expected it would be like to move through some grief today?”
Okay, while juxtapositions often can naturally emerge after STEP 4 (probably
do so about half the time), they don’t always do so. So we will discuss how to
actively search for juxtapositions next!

IDEAS FOR DISCONFIRMATIONS
As mentioned, about half the time, when the client simply states the contents
of their schema with full awareness and consciousness in session--doing so
immediately triggers a ‘wait a minute! I know that isn’t true!’. Not of the
cognitive kind, but of actual deeply held knowing that feels true enough to
serve as a disconfirmation. In that case, the therapist would immediately want
to ask the client to expand upon this ‘other’ knowing, and then serve both the
old and the new ‘aha’ together at the same time (thereby creating a
juxtaposition).
However, if this doesn’t happen, then the therapist needs to consider what kind
of experience, or imagery, or line of questioning, might help a disconfirmation
come into awareness.
All sorts of options for creating disconfirmations are outlined in the
Coherence Therapy manual. They can be as simple as asking if the client
has ever had an experience where things didn’t go as expected by the
schema.
“Have you ever had an experience when XYZ didn’t happen? (...you honestly
expressed a need to som﻿eone and they didn’t shame you for it?)”

STEP 5: Juxtaposition

JUXTAPOSITION EXPERIENCE
Creating a juxtaposition is quite simple. In fact, all the hard work is simply in
getting to the point where a true juxtaposition can be made...with the two core
ingredients being two, subjectively real-feeling truths (the schema, and the
disconfirmation), that both cannot be true at the same time.
This template is often useful:
“Let’s go over two things that seem to feel true to you. If you will, allow yourself
to actually feel these things as we review them. First, what you learned in life
very deeply is that (review the contents of the schema as captured by the overt
statement), and second, that you’ve had experiences that clearly show you
XYZ“ (ie, “and second, that you just now are seeing that you can fully touch
into the anger, and actually stay grounded at the same time).”
As you set up this experience, you aren’t favoring one over the other (“you
used to think this false thing, but now you see the truth...”). No, you want to
leave judgement out of it, and simply hold both knowings side by side, trusting
their brain to register the mismatch.
Then you might simply ask, “How is it to be in touch with both?” It is important
to review the juxtaposition, in its specificity, at least twice more during the next
minutes, which is usually easy to do quite naturally.
If you are doing IFS work, you could easily do this using parts language. “So
some part of you has always believed XYZ, but now you can clearly see ABC.”
For instance: “Wow. So this sweet boy is really showing you how it was his
fault that he got teased. But from your perspective, you can really now see how
it wasn’t his fault at all.” Or “Yes, let’s bring back that protector part we started
with. The one who always knew it was absolutely urgent to make sure you
were never made to feel small again. Let’s let that part witness that young part
now, and how he knows he’s good and enough. Can the part see that?”

THOUGHTS ON INTEGRATING BETWEEN MODELS
Before we end, I would like to offer some reflections on the strategy of
integrating between models, versus fully mastering one model. I personally so
deeply enjoy learning that I can’t hold myself back from learning many
approaches. And then consequently my teaching becomes integrative,
because I teach what know. I also lean toward integration because when the
goal is memory reconsolidation, it can certainly be useful to have a wide
variety of tools (or even frameworks) from which you can pull from and
integrate as needed. In other words, there is potentially something quite
wonderful about the idea of using the brain’s requirement for memory
reconsolidation as the overarching frame, and then drawing on techniques to
support those requirements.
That being said, one could make a strong case for specializing in one (or
maybe two) theoretical approaches. In fact, after my students complete my
core training (which hopefully introduces them to the unique strengths of some
major types of experiential therapy), my advice to my students is to pick a
‘theoretical home,’ i.e. a model that can serve as their home-base. And this is
the advice I would like to give to you. While I would love you to feel free to use
this manual as a crash-course in some of the experiential basics, and as a
frame for integrating between various approaches you have already learned
aspects of, at the same time I think it can be incredibly helpful to learn at least
one theory fully--from top to bottom. If you haven’t yet done so then perhaps
reading this manual will have helped you identify which way of working most
speaks to your soul and aligns with your values or strengths.
When discussing this with my students, I like to point out that there are two
main archetypes we therapists can fit into. One is that of a midwife, and one is
that of a detective (or scientist). I encourage my students to think about which
more fits their personality / passion. Some have expressed really loving
showing up like a midwife, where their presence and attunement allows a
natural movement inside the client’s system (i.e. the movement toward
completion or emotional processing). And others have been more drawn to the
idea of being a detective, where they are bringing their curiosity and ‘Socratic
Questions’ to the mission of outlining schemas (getting to the ‘why’ the client is
stuck in the
